The question

Can dividing an asset’s resale value change the price of the asset itself?

Different investors may be the most optimistic buyers of different tranches. Their separate valuations feed back into the asset’s resale price.

The examples below show how this feedback can raise prices and generate more than one equilibrium without changing dividends or beliefs.

The motivating example · Section 2

Two claims on the same resale value

The senior tranche receives up to the attachment point. The junior tranche receives what is left. Their payoffs add up to the asset’s next-period resale price.

01 · Divide the resale value

Senior first. Junior receives the remainder.

For an attachment point of 2.25 and a resale price of q, the senior payoff is min(q, 2.25). The junior payoff is max(q − 2.25, 0).

02 · Price the claims separately

Different buyers value different parts most.

In the limiting example, theory A assigns equal probabilities to all three next-period states. Theory B assigns probability one to staying in the current state. At current state m, B values the senior tranche more and A values the junior tranche more.

At the tranching equilibrium, the resale prices in states (l, m, h) are (1.05, 2.25, 3.00). The table shows each theory’s expected tranche payoff discounted by the gross return of 2.

Willingness to pay at state m

Each tranche is priced at its highest valuation. The row maxima can come from different theories.

03 · Feed the security prices back into the asset

The asset price includes the value of issuing the claims.

At state m, the discounted declared dividend is 2 / 2 = 1. The senior and junior prices are 1.125 and 0.125. Together they support an asset price of 1 + 1.125 + 0.125 = 2.25, compared with 2 without tranching.

The same equilibrium resale prices determine tomorrow’s tranche payoffs. An equilibrium must satisfy both sides of this feedback.

Check the equilibrium prices and the underlying beliefs

Theory A assigns probability 1/3 to each next-period state at every current state. Theory B uses the transition matrix below, which is also the objective process. All its entries are positive.

The three vectors are (848, 930, 978) / 327, (259, 285, 297) / 97, and (2201, 2425, 2523) / 803. They are known fixed points from Example 1, not the output of a procedure that finds every equilibrium.

At each current state, add the highest discounted valuation of each tranche to the discounted declared dividend. The result is the underlying asset price. Zero-payoff tranches can have tied valuations.

Without tranching

The same economy has a unique equilibrium, (2.593, 2.844, 2.991), equal to the lowest of the three vectors above. Here, adding a senior and a junior tranche creates multiplicity. This is an example, not a claim that every tranching creates multiple equilibria.

Finding the lowest and highest equilibria

Apply the price-forming operator repeatedly. The lower sequence starts at zero and rises. The upper sequence starts at the bound from Proposition 1 and falls. Their limits are the least and greatest equilibrium price functions.

What the solver does

lower = zero price in every state
upper = M_T in every state
repeat for each sequence until the stopping rule is met:
    next = price_operator(economy, current)
return lower limit, upper limit

This is a guide to the algorithm, not a replacement for the executable implementation. The code accepts at most three states and three tranches. Numerical agreement of the extremes is not, by itself, a proof of uniqueness.

Aggregate realized excess payoffs in the paper are defined using unit tranche purchases and objective long-run frequencies. They are not a measure of total welfare or actual portfolio holdings.
