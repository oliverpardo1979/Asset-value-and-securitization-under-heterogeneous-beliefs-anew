I thank the editor for the careful reading of the revised manuscript and for the constructive requests in the minor revision decision. I have addressed all four points. The principal changes are a complete-lattice characterization of the equilibrium set, a simpler multiplicity example, an explicit set-valued refinement comparison, and a new uniqueness result under the spectral condition. I also reviewed the manuscript paragraph by paragraph and clarified the disclosure concerning the use of AI. Table~\ref{tab:manuscript-changes} reproduces the original and revised passages in manuscript order, bringing together revisions to results, proofs, exposition, and terminology.

\commenttext{Add the uniqueness implication of the spectral-radius condition. This is discussed in Reviewer 2's report.}

\response{Proposition 9 now states and proves that, when all eigenvalues of $A$ lie strictly inside the unit circle, every finite tranching has a unique waterfall-equilibrium price. For any two equilibrium price functions $q$ and $w$, the proof establishes
\[
|q-w|\leq A^n|q-w|
\]
for every $n\geq1$. The eigenvalue condition implies that $A^n$ converges to zero, so $q=w$. The proposition also retains the upper bound provided by the state-contingent price.

The discussion following Proposition 9 explains that $A$ depends on beliefs and interest rates, not on the tranching. When all its eigenvalues lie strictly inside the unit circle, tranching may still raise prices but cannot generate multiplicity.}

\commenttext{Clarify the role and scope of the least-equilibrium selection. The statement that the comparative statics ``require an equilibrium selection'' is too broad: set-valued comparative statics are possible without selecting an equilibrium. The intended point is that the paper's single-valued price comparisons require a selection. Please revise the wording accordingly.}

\response{The paper now clarifies that equilibrium sets can be compared without selecting a particular equilibrium, whereas comparisons based on a single equilibrium price function for each tranching require specifying a selection. The least-equilibrium selection is used to define and characterize issuer optimality, rather than imposed throughout the paper. The finite-horizon result is retained as the motivation for selecting the least equilibrium.

Lemma 1 has been strengthened to state the set-valued comparison. If $\mathcal S$ refines $\mathcal T$, then every equilibrium under $\mathcal T$ is weakly below some equilibrium under $\mathcal S$, and every equilibrium under $\mathcal S$ is weakly above some equilibrium under $\mathcal T$. The proof follows by comparing the two price operators and iterating from an arbitrary fixed point. As consequences, both the least and the greatest equilibrium prices weakly increase under refinement.

The comparisons with the no-tranching benchmark and perceived fundamental values, as well as the iterated-securitization equivalence, now explicitly cover all equilibria. Proposition 6 now establishes that the aggregate realized excess payoff is nonpositive in every waterfall equilibrium, not only the least equilibrium. Proposition 7 likewise applies to every equilibrium: when one theory has the finest information partition, its realized excess payoff is zero and those of all other theories are nonpositive. Corollary 5 now shows that refinement weakly reduces the aggregate realized excess payoff not only when comparing the least equilibria before and after refinement, but also when comparing the greatest equilibria before and after refinement.

The new Corollary 4 uses the existing aggregate-payoff identity to compare equilibria for a given tranching. The aggregate realized excess payoff is highest at the least equilibrium and lowest at the greatest equilibrium, with a strict difference whenever there is more than one equilibrium. Individual theories' excess payoffs need not follow the same ranking.}

\commenttext{Please describe more generally the structure of the equilibrium set and the robustness of multiplicity, and simplify Example 1 if possible. The report suggests a two-tranche example with attachment point 3 and supplies exact fixed points.}

\response{Proposition 1 now uses Tarski's fixed-point theorem to show that, for every finite tranching, the entire set of waterfall-equilibrium prices is a nonempty complete lattice under the pointwise order. Thus every collection of equilibrium prices has an infimum and a supremum that are themselves equilibria.

Example 1 now uses the simpler two-tranche economy suggested in the decision letter. It has three states, $X=\{l,m,h\}$, with
\[
P=\frac18
\begin{pmatrix}
4&1&3\\
1&6&1\\
3&1&4
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
R=\frac{13}{12},
\qquad
d=\frac1{12}(0,3,5).
\]
The coarse theory assigns probability $1/3$ to each next-period state, while the fine theory uses $P$. The tranching is $\mathcal T=\{[0,3),[3,\infty)\}$. I verified that the three reported price vectors satisfy the equilibrium conditions by direct substitution. The main text presents both their exact and approximate values and explains when residual equity pays in each equilibrium: never in the first, only in state $h$ in the second, and in states $m$ and $h$ in the third. The text also identifies the first equilibrium as the least equilibrium, the second as an intermediate equilibrium, and the third as the greatest equilibrium. The least equilibrium coincides with the unique equilibrium price without tranching.

Example 1 now includes a brief local-robustness observation: sufficiently small changes in the transition probabilities, dividends, interest rate, and attachment point preserve three distinct equilibria. A detailed proof would require setting out and analyzing the local equilibrium systems for all three cases. I have kept the discussion brief to avoid substantially lengthening the example. I have nevertheless verified local robustness separately for each of the three equilibria.}
